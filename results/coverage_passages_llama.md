Where, during summer and other seasons, water comes by way of rivers, canals, etc., and is lifted into the fields, agricultural work is then praised. Fields in the world are divided into two types according to their natural character—along riverbanks, in villages, and inside forests, on mountain slopes, and on uneven low and elevated grounds. Among these, the first, śāli (paddy) land, is declared supreme by the sages. The second type, āḍhaka (pulse) land, is medium yet fruitful. Śāli land, however, is naturally abundant in water. It is known for complete mud and extremely soft soil, intertwined with major and minor canals and branches flowing with water. It is surrounded on all sides by small protective mud dikes, situated near threshing floors or near village residential areas. The condition of śāli-land is determined by the alignment of the fields, being low-lying in some places towards the east and in others towards the west. Sometimes it is low-lying on the south, or has low ground towards the north; occasionally it is mostly level with water channels directed to receive water. From another field, there is sometimes a stream of water fit to enter the field's surface; thus the various characteristics of śāli-land are defined. From the first plowing up to the production of grain on this earth, śāli-land well-supplied with water is renowned for yielding complete harvest. The second type of land, elevated ground situated near village dwelling sites, at forest fringes, or on banks, is designated as āḍhaka-land (for pulse crops). Sustained even by small drops of water, it promotes the growth of chickpea (caṇaka) and pigeon pea (āḍhaka); constructing water channels for it is not prescribed. It generally becomes suitable for plowing with a small sprinkling of water, requiring only slight absorption of water at the time of sowing seeds.
